# Round 9 - Behavioral and Project: Behavioral, Project Deep Dive and Questions to Ask - Answers

**Questions:** [../questions/01-behavioral-and-project.md](../questions/01-behavioral-and-project.md)

## Read this first: the STAR method and how these answers work

Behavioral questions ask for a real story from your past ("Tell me about a time..."). A story is easy to follow when it has four parts. The parts are called **STAR**:

```text
S - Situation (1 or 2 sentences): where, when, what was at stake.
T - Task (1 sentence): what YOU had to achieve.
A - Action (3 to 5 sentences, the longest part): what YOU did, step by step, and why.
R - Result (1 or 2 sentences): the outcome, with a number or a clear fact, and what you learned.
```

Rules that make a STAR answer strong:
- Aim for 90 seconds to 2 minutes. Spend most of the time on the Action.
- Say "I" for what you did and "we" only for the team context. Interviewers want to know your part.
- Use only true facts and real numbers. Interviewers ask follow-up questions, and an invented detail falls apart quickly. If you have no number, describe a clear before and after instead.
- End with what you learned or what you would do differently. This matters most for failure and conflict questions.
- Never blame a person. Talk about the problem, the facts and your own choices.

**Important about the model answers below.** They are **example outlines with placeholders in square brackets** (for example `[your project]`). They are not real stories and they contain no real numbers. Each one is labelled "example, replace with your story". Copy the shape, not the words, and fill the placeholders with your own history. Do not read them out loud as they are.

Here is the shape once, in full (example, replace with your story):

```text
Question: Tell me about a time you improved something under pressure.
S: On [your project], [a page or feature] was [slow / confusing / failing] for [a type of user].
T: I was responsible for [fixing it / deciding what to do] before [a date or event].
A: First I [measured or reproduced the problem]. Then I [list 2 or 3 concrete steps and the reason for
   each]. When [a difficulty] came up, I [what you decided]. I told [people] about [the plan or risk].
R: [The measurable outcome, for example "page load fell from [X] to [Y]"]. I learned [one lesson],
   and now I [one habit you changed].
```

If you do not have a good story for a question, say so honestly and use the closest real one. Plan this in advance with the story bank in the [Round 9 README](../README.md).

## Behavioral: introductions

### R9-BH-001 (Easy) Walk me through your background
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your story) Keep it to about two minutes, in three parts: past, present, future.
- Past: "I started in [first role or study] and learned [core skill]. Then I moved into [frontend work] because [real reason]."
- Present: "Now I work as [title] at [your company], where I build [type of product] with React and [two or three main tools]. My main contribution is [one thing you own]."
- Future: "I am looking for [what you want next, for example larger scope or more ownership of architecture]. That is why this role caught my eye."

Pick the points that match the job description. Leave out unrelated jobs and personal details.
**Why it matters / follow-up:** It sets the story the interviewer will probe. Expect "tell me more about [the thing you mentioned]". Only mention what you can discuss in depth.
**Learn more:** [How React interviews work](../../../00-start-here/03-how-react-interviews-work.md)

### R9-BH-002 (Easy) Why do you want to work at this company, and why frontend work?
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your story) Give one reason about the company, one about the work and one about yourself.
- Company: "I read about [a real product, a public blog post, a feature you used or a company value]. What interests me is [a specific detail]." Do the research before the interview.
- Frontend: "I like frontend because the result is visible to users within minutes. I enjoy [state, accessibility, performance, design systems: pick what is true for you]."
- You: "My experience with [skill] fits your need for [something from the job description]."

Do not give generic praise ("great culture") or a reason that is only about pay.
**Why it matters / follow-up:** The interviewer checks motivation and whether you read about them. A follow-up may be "what would you change about our product?" Prepare one respectful, specific observation.

## Behavioral: working with people

### R9-BH-003 (Medium) Tell me about a conflict with a teammate
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your story)
- **S:** "On [project], a teammate and I disagreed about [a specific technical or process matter, for example how to structure a shared component]."
- **T:** "We had to agree before [a milestone] so that the work did not block others."
- **A:** "I first asked questions to understand their reasons. Then I wrote down both options with their costs. We looked at facts: [a small prototype, an existing guideline, a measurement]. [If it fits: I asked a third person to give an opinion.] We chose [decision], and I wrote it in [the team doc]."
- **R:** "We shipped on time and kept a good relationship. I learned to separate the idea from the person and to settle debates with evidence."

Choose a real conflict that you resolved calmly. Do not choose a story where the other person is simply "the problem".
**Why it matters / follow-up:** Interviewers look for empathy, directness and a result. Follow-up: "What would you do if they still disagreed?" (Answer: escalate the decision to the tech lead with both options and accept the outcome.)
**Learn more:** [How React interviews work](../../../00-start-here/03-how-react-interviews-work.md)

### R9-BH-004 (Medium) Tell me about a time you disagreed with a designer
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your story)
- **S:** "Our designer proposed [a design element, for example a custom animated dropdown] for [a screen]."
- **T:** "I had to build it, and I saw a risk in [accessibility, performance, effort, behaviour on small screens]."
- **A:** "I did not just say no. I explained the risk in user terms: [for example keyboard users could not open it]. I made a quick prototype or a list of options with effort estimates. I proposed [an alternative that kept the goal of the design]. We reviewed it together and agreed on [decision]."
- **R:** "We shipped [outcome]. I learned to bring options, not objections, and to talk about the user's goal."

Show that you respect design skill and that you speak early, before the build.
**Why it matters / follow-up:** Frontend engineers work with designers daily. The interviewer checks collaboration and user focus. Follow-up: "How do you handle a design that is hard to build?" (Raise it early, offer an alternative, and agree on a design handoff process.)
**Learn more:** [Accessibility](../../../04-advanced/09-accessibility.md)

### R9-BH-005 (Medium) A product manager asks for a feature by Friday and you think it cannot be done well in that time
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your story) The key skill is to negotiate scope with facts, not to say yes or no.
- **S:** "On [project], [a PM] asked for [feature] by [date]."
- **T:** "I had to deliver value without hurting quality, and tell the truth about the risk."
- **A:** "I broke the feature into pieces and estimated each one, with the unknowns marked. I showed the PM three options: (1) a smaller first version on the date, (2) the full version a little later, (3) the full version on the date with named risks such as less testing. I recommended one and said why. We agreed to ship [the smaller version] first behind a feature flag."
- **R:** "We hit the date with [the agreed scope] and delivered the rest in [a later release]. I learned to bring a recommendation, not only a problem."

**Why it matters / follow-up:** It tests communication under pressure. Follow-up: "What if the PM insists on the full scope?" (Explain the risk clearly, ask who accepts it, record the decision, and reduce risk with tests and a rollback plan.)
**Learn more:** [How React interviews work](../../../00-start-here/03-how-react-interviews-work.md)

### R9-BH-006 (Medium) Working with a backend team on an API that did not fit your screen
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your story)
- **S:** "The API for [a screen] returned [a shape that needed many requests, was missing fields, or had unclear errors]."
- **T:** "I needed a contract both teams could rely on, without slowing the release."
- **A:** "I wrote down what the screen needs (fields, sorting, paging, error cases) and met the backend engineer. We agreed on a short contract (an OpenAPI file or a shared type definition, whichever your team uses) and on error formats and status codes. Until the real endpoint was ready, I used a mock server so I could keep building. I added one test that checks the real response against the agreed shape."
- **R:** "[Outcome: fewer requests, fewer integration bugs, a quicker release]. I learned to agree on the contract before building."

**Why it matters / follow-up:** It shows you can work across teams. Follow-up: "What do you do when the API changes without warning?" (Version it, add contract tests, and ask for change notices.)
**Learn more:** [Data fetching](../../../03-intermediate/02-data-fetching.md)

### R9-BH-007 (Medium) Code review feedback you disagreed with at first
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your story)
- **S:** "A reviewer asked me to change [something in my pull request, for example split a large component or remove a custom hook]."
- **T:** "I had to decide whether to accept it, without taking it personally."
- **A:** "I read the comment twice and asked a question to be sure I understood the reason. I checked it against [the team guideline or the code]. [If they were right: I changed it and thanked them. If I still disagreed: I explained my reasoning with an example, and we agreed on a middle way or asked a third person.]"
- **R:** "[Result: a cleaner pull request, a new team rule, or a lesson]. I learned that review is about the code, not about me."

Say honestly if you were wrong. That is a strength.
**Why it matters / follow-up:** It checks humility and the ability to disagree politely. Follow-up: "Do you ever ignore feedback?" (Only after a discussion, and I write down why, for example in the pull request thread.)
**Learn more:** [How React interviews work](../../../00-start-here/03-how-react-interviews-work.md)

### R9-BH-008 (Medium) How do you give hard feedback in a code review?
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your story) State your approach, then show a real example.
- Approach: be specific, comment on the code and not on the person, explain the "why", and suggest an alternative. Mark what is a blocking problem and what is only a suggestion (for example with a "nit:" prefix for small style points). Ask a question when you are not sure ("What happens if the list is empty?").
- Example: "I reviewed a pull request where [a problem, for example an effect that copied props into state]. I wrote [your actual comment: the problem, the reason, a small fix]. I offered to pair for ten minutes. The author [result]."
- Also praise good work. Say so when something is clean.

**Why it matters / follow-up:** Seniors are expected to raise the quality of others without hurting morale. Follow-up: "What do you do with a large pull request?" (Ask to split it, or review in passes: design first, then details.)
**Learn more:** [How React interviews work](../../../00-start-here/03-how-react-interviews-work.md)

### R9-BH-009 (Medium) Tell me about a time you mentored a junior engineer
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your story)
- **S:** "A new teammate joined [team] and struggled with [a concept, for example effects, state or the project's folder structure]."
- **T:** "I wanted them to work independently within [a time frame]."
- **A:** "I set up a weekly one-to-one. I gave a small first task in a safe area of the code. I explained the reason behind rules and pointed to code examples. In reviews I asked questions so they found the answer themselves. We paired on one hard problem, and I let them drive."
- **R:** "They [concrete outcome, for example shipped their first feature alone in [time]]. I learned that explaining a topic is the fastest way to learn it myself."

If you have not mentored anyone formally, use a real smaller case: onboarding a teammate, a lunch talk, or answering questions in a chat channel. Say it honestly.
**Why it matters / follow-up:** Mid and senior roles include growing others. Follow-up: "What do you do when the person is not improving?" (Give clear, kind feedback, break goals into small steps, and ask the lead for help if needed.)
**Learn more:** [How React interviews work](../../../00-start-here/03-how-react-interviews-work.md)

### R9-BH-010 (Hard) A time you pushed back on a decision, then committed
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your story) This is "disagree and commit". Show both halves.
- **S:** "The team (or my manager) decided to [a decision, for example adopt a library or skip a refactor]."
- **T:** "I thought it carried a risk: [the risk]."
- **A:** "I raised it in [the right place, for example the design review] with evidence: [a measurement, a prototype or a past incident]. I listened to their reasons. The decision stayed. Then I committed fully: I helped implement it and did not say 'I told you so'. I also suggested a small check for the risk, for example a metric to watch."
- **R:** "[What happened: you were wrong, they were wrong, or a mix]. I learned [lesson]."

**Why it matters / follow-up:** It shows courage and team spirit together. Follow-up: "When do you not commit?" (When the decision is unsafe or breaks law or user trust; then escalate openly.)
**Learn more:** [How React interviews work](../../../00-start-here/03-how-react-interviews-work.md)

### R9-BH-011 (Hard) Leading a change without formal authority
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your story)
- **S:** "I noticed [a recurring problem, for example slow builds, no shared components, flaky tests] across [teams]."
- **T:** "Nobody owned it, and I had no authority over the other teams."
- **A:** "I collected evidence (time lost, bug counts). I wrote a short proposal and shared it with the people affected. I started with a small pilot in one team to show value. I asked for feedback and changed the plan. I wrote documentation and held a short demo."
- **R:** "[Adoption and measured result]. I learned that influence comes from evidence, small steps and listening."

**Why it matters / follow-up:** It is a staff-level signal. Follow-up: "What if people ignored it?" (Find out why, fix the real blocker, and find one sponsor.)
**Learn more:** [Frontend system design](../../../04-advanced/11-frontend-system-design.md)

## Behavioral: delivery and ownership

### R9-BH-012 (Medium) Something you drove from the idea to production
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your story) Show that you own the whole path, not only the code.
- **S:** "[Problem or opportunity] with [user impact]."
- **T:** "I owned [feature] from [first step] to [launch]."
- **A:** "I wrote the plan and asked questions to clarify requirements. I built [parts], coordinated with [design, backend, QA], added tests and monitoring, and prepared a rollback or a feature flag. After launch I watched [metrics or errors] and fixed [issues]."
- **R:** "[Result with a number or a clear fact]. I learned [lesson]."

**Why it matters / follow-up:** Companies hire for ownership. Follow-up: "What would you do differently?" Have an honest answer.
**Learn more:** [How React interviews work](../../../00-start-here/03-how-react-interviews-work.md)

### R9-BH-013 (Medium) A tight deadline: estimating and cutting scope
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your story)
- **S:** "We had [a deadline tied to something real: a launch, a contract, an event] and [the work looked bigger than the time]."
- **T:** "Deliver the most value on time."
- **A:** "I listed the tasks and split them into must-have and nice-to-have with the product owner. I gave estimates as ranges and named the unknowns. I cut [nice-to-have parts], planned them for a later release, and put risky parts first. I did not cut tests for the critical path. I gave short progress updates."
- **R:** "We shipped [must-haves] on time, and [what followed]. I learned to flag risk early."

**Why it matters / follow-up:** It checks planning and honesty. Follow-up: "Do you work overtime to hit deadlines?" (Be truthful: occasionally for a real peak, but I fix the plan first.)
**Learn more:** [How React interviews work](../../../00-start-here/03-how-react-interviews-work.md)

### R9-BH-014 (Medium) A task with unclear requirements
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your story)
- **S:** "I got a request like '[a vague request, for example make the dashboard better]'."
- **T:** "Turn it into something buildable."
- **A:** "I asked who the user is, what problem they have and how we will know it worked. I wrote down my assumptions and the open questions, and sent them to the owner. I built a small version or a clickable prototype to get quick feedback. I decided what to leave out, and recorded what was agreed."
- **R:** "[Result, for example fewer rework cycles]. I learned that early questions are cheaper than late rewrites."

This is the same habit as the clarifying-questions step in a live coding or system design round.
**Why it matters / follow-up:** Real work is vague. Follow-up: "What if nobody can answer your questions?" (Choose the safest assumption, say it out loud, and make it easy to change.)
**Learn more:** [How React interviews work](../../../00-start-here/03-how-react-interviews-work.md)

### R9-BH-015 (Medium) Balancing technical debt and new features
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your story) Explain your rule, then a real case.
- Rule: "Debt is a loan. I accept it knowingly when speed matters, and I pay it back where it slows us down most." I connect debt to cost: slow builds, repeated bugs, hard onboarding.
- Case: "On [project], [a messy area] caused [a measurable cost]. I made the case with [data]. We agreed to [a small refactor attached to a feature that touched that area], or a fixed share of each sprint. I avoided a big-bang rewrite."
- Result: "[Outcome]. I learned to describe debt in business terms."

**Why it matters / follow-up:** It checks judgement. Follow-up: "When do you choose a rewrite?" (Rarely. Prefer step-by-step refactors with tests, because rewrites often take longer than planned.)
**Learn more:** [Performance optimization](../../../04-advanced/02-performance-optimization.md)

### R9-BH-016 (Medium) Your biggest professional failure
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your story) Pick a real failure that is not fatal and not a hidden strength ("I work too hard" is not a failure).
- **S/T:** "On [project], I was responsible for [task]."
- **A (what went wrong):** "I [honest description of your mistake, for example skipped testing a risky change, underestimated the work, or did not ask for help]." Own it without excuses.
- **A (what you did):** "I told [people] quickly, helped fix it, and [fixed the damage]."
- **R:** "[Real impact]. Since then I [a concrete habit or process change]. For example [a checklist, a test, an earlier question]."

The last part matters most: show that you changed.
**Why it matters / follow-up:** It checks self-awareness. Follow-up: "Has the same thing happened again?" Be ready with an honest answer.
**Learn more:** [How React interviews work](../../../00-start-here/03-how-react-interviews-work.md)

### R9-BH-017 (Hard) A production bug you caused or fixed, and what the postmortem said
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your story)
- **S:** "After release [version or feature], users saw [symptom, for example a blank page for users with an empty cart]."
- **T:** "Restore service first, then find the cause."
- **A:** "First, stop the damage: [roll back / turn off the feature flag / hotfix]. Then find the cause: I reproduced it, read the error report [and used source maps to read the stack trace], and found [root cause]. I added a test that fails without the fix. Afterwards we held a blameless postmortem, a meeting about what went wrong in the system and process, not about who is to blame."
- **R:** "[Time to recover]. Follow-up actions: [for example a test, an alert, a checklist item], each with an owner. I learned [lesson]."

Good postmortem content: timeline, impact, root cause, what went well, what to improve, action items.
**Why it matters / follow-up:** It tests calm under pressure and learning. Follow-up: "How did you find out about the bug?" (Say whether it was an alert, a user, or by luck, and what you improved in detection.)
**Learn more:** [Error boundaries, portals and friends](../../../03-intermediate/05-error-boundaries-portals-and-friends.md)

### R9-BH-018 (Hard) A frontend release is broken in production: how do you handle it?
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your story) Give a clear order of steps. Then add a real example if you have one.
1. **Confirm and size it.** Check monitoring and error reports. Who is affected, how many, how bad?
2. **Tell people.** Post in the incident channel and name who leads. One person coordinates, others investigate.
3. **Stop the damage first.** Choose the fastest safe option: roll back to the last good build, turn off the feature with a feature flag, or ship a small hotfix. Rollback is often faster than debugging live.
4. **Find the root cause** after users are safe. Reproduce, read the stack trace, compare with the last release.
5. **Fix and verify.** Add a test, release gradually if you can, and watch the metrics.
6. **Learn.** Hold a blameless postmortem and add actions (alerts, tests, release checks).

Frontend specifics: cached old JavaScript files, a CDN, and users who keep a tab open for days can make a bug last longer than the rollback.
**Why it matters / follow-up:** It checks priorities: users first, root cause second. Follow-up: "How would you know before users complain?" (Error monitoring, alerts on error rate, and Web Vitals from real users.)
**Learn more:** [Frontend system design](../../../04-advanced/11-frontend-system-design.md)

## Behavioral: growth, quality and judgement

### R9-BH-019 (Easy) Learning a new tool or library quickly
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your story)
- **S/T:** "My team needed [tool or library] for [project] within [time], and I did not know it."
- **A:** "I read the official documentation first and built a small throwaway project to test the main ideas. I asked a colleague who knew it for a short review of my first code. I wrote down what I learned for the team. Then I used it on a small real task before the big one."
- **R:** "[Outcome and time]. I learned that building a tiny project beats only reading."

**Why it matters / follow-up:** Tools change quickly. Follow-up: "How do you know you understand it well enough?" (I can explain it to someone else and say when not to use it.)
**Learn more:** [How React interviews work](../../../00-start-here/03-how-react-interviews-work.md)

### R9-BH-020 (Easy) Keeping up with the ecosystem without chasing every trend
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your story) Say what you really do. For example: "I follow the official React blog and release notes, [one or two newsletters or people you actually read], and I try new things in small side projects. Before using a new tool at work I ask: what problem does it solve for us, how mature is it, and what does it cost to leave it later?" A good sign is a recent example: "Recently I learned [a real new feature, for example the React 19 form Actions] by [what you did]."
**Why it matters / follow-up:** The interviewer checks curiosity and good judgement, not a list of famous names. Follow-up: "What is a new trend you decided not to adopt, and why?"
**Learn more:** [React 19 and newer](../../../04-advanced/04-react-19-and-newer.md)

### R9-BH-021 (Medium) How do you make sure the code you ship is good?
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your story) Describe layers, then say which ones you really used.
- **Before the pull request:** types (TypeScript), linting, formatting, and tests close to the code.
- **In the pull request:** small changes, a review by another person, and automatic checks in CI (lint, type check, tests, build).
- **Tests:** unit tests for logic, component tests that act like a user (Testing Library), and a few end-to-end tests for the main flows.
- **After release:** error monitoring, alerts, and Web Vitals from real users. Gradual rollout and feature flags where they fit.
- Add one concrete case: "On [project], we caught [problem] with [layer]."

**Why it matters / follow-up:** It shows engineering maturity. Follow-up: "What is your minimum for a small bug fix?" (A test that fails before and passes after.)
**Learn more:** [Testing React](../../../04-advanced/08-testing-react.md)

### R9-BH-022 (Medium) How do you use AI coding tools, and how do you check what they give you?
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your story) Be honest about what you do and about company policy.
- Use: "I use them for [boilerplate, test ideas, explaining unfamiliar code, first drafts], if my company allows it." If you do not use them, say so and say why, and show you have an opinion.
- Check: "I never paste code I cannot explain. I read every line, run the code, run the tests, and think about edge cases. I check APIs against the official documentation, because tools can suggest functions that do not exist or are out of date. I keep secrets and private code out of tools that my company has not approved."
- Judgement: "The tool does not own the result. I do. In review, I explain my choices as if I wrote every line."

**Why it matters / follow-up:** Interviewers want productivity with responsibility. Follow-up: "Tell me about a time the tool was wrong." Prepare one real example. For how to behave when a tool is allowed in the interview itself, see the lesson.
**Learn more:** [How React interviews work](../../../00-start-here/03-how-react-interviews-work.md)

### R9-BH-023 (Medium) A time you improved accessibility in a product

- **S:** "[A screen or component] could not be used with [a keyboard or a screen reader], found by [an audit, a user report or a tool]."

### R9-BH-024 (Hard) A technical decision you now regret

- **S:** "I chose [technology or design] for [project], because [reasons that were reasonable then]."

## Project deep dive

For these questions, the answer is your own real project. The outlines show the structure and the details a good answer includes. Only describe what you truly did. If a topic was handled by another team or you do not know the detail, say so and explain what you understand.

### R9-PJ-001 (Medium) Walk me through the architecture of your last project
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your project) Go from the outside in, in about three minutes.
1. **Purpose and users:** "It is a [type of product] for [users]. Scale: [real numbers if you know them, such as users, pages, team size]."
2. **Main screens and flow:** "The key screens are [list]. A typical user flow is [one flow]."
3. **Frontend structure:** "React [version] with [Vite or a framework], [TypeScript or not], components grouped by [feature or type]."
4. **Data and state:** "Server data comes from [REST or GraphQL] through [fetch, a data library]. Shared client state lives in [store or context]. Form and URL state: [tools]."
5. **Rendering and delivery:** "[Client-only, server rendering or static]. Deployed with [pipeline] to [hosting]."
6. **Quality and operations:** "Tests: [kinds]. Monitoring: [tools]. Accessibility and performance practices: [one line each]."

Draw it on paper or a whiteboard if you can: boxes for browser, API and services.
**Why it matters / follow-up:** It proves you understood the whole system, not only your ticket. Follow-up: "Why was it built this way?" and "Which part was your idea?"
**Learn more:** [Frontend system design](../../../04-advanced/11-frontend-system-design.md)

### R9-PJ-002 (Easy) Your exact role on the project
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your project) Separate three things clearly: "I built", "I helped with" and "others built".
- "I owned [feature or area] from design discussion to release."
- "I contributed to [shared parts] by [reviews, bug fixes, documentation]."
- "Other people built [parts], and I worked with them through [the interface between us]."

Be precise about team size and your seniority on the project. Know the details of everything you claim: interviewers compare your story with your CV and probe it.
**Why it matters / follow-up:** The most common red flag is a vague "we did everything". Follow-up: "Show me the code you are proudest of" or "What would your teammates say about your work?"
**Learn more:** [How React interviews work](../../../00-start-here/03-how-react-interviews-work.md)

### R9-PJ-003 (Medium) Why did you pick React and your main libraries, and what were the alternatives?
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your project) If you did not choose, say who did and what you understood about the reasons. Strong answers name criteria:
- Team skills and hiring pool, the ecosystem and its maturity, maintenance and release history, bundle size, TypeScript support, documentation, and the cost of leaving the tool later.
- Example shape: "We chose [state or data library] because [a reason tied to the problem, for example most of our state was server data]. We considered [alternative] but it [real trade-off]."
- Be fair to alternatives. Say what the trade-off was: "Angular, Vue or Svelte could also work. We picked React because [your real reason]."

**Why it matters / follow-up:** It shows engineering judgement, not fashion. Follow-up: "Would you pick the same today?"
**Learn more:** [What is React](../../../01-basics/02-what-is-react.md)

### R9-PJ-004 (Medium) How did you handle state?
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your project) Sort state by kind and say where each lives.
- **Local UI state** (open/closed, input text): `useState` or `useReducer` inside the component.
- **Shared client state** (theme, a cart, a wizard): lifted to the nearest common parent, then Context or a store library only when needed.
- **Server state** (data from the API): a data-fetching library such as TanStack Query, which handles caching, loading and error states.
- **URL state** (filters, page, selected tab): in the URL, so links and the back button work.
- **Form state:** a form library or plain controlled inputs.

Add one trade-off you met, for example: "Context caused extra re-renders when the value changed often, so we [what you did]." Say what you really used.
**Why it matters / follow-up:** It shows the "state is not one thing" mindset. Follow-up: "Why not put everything in Redux?" or "How do you avoid copying server data into state?"
**Learn more:** [State management](../../../03-intermediate/06-state-management.md)

### R9-PJ-005 (Medium) How did you fetch, cache and update server data?
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your project)
- **Fetching:** "[Tool] fetches data. Each request has loading, error and empty states."
- **Caching:** "Results are cached by a key (for example the resource and its parameters), shown immediately when they are fresh enough, and refreshed in the background when they are stale."
- **Updates:** "After a change (a mutation), we either refetch the affected data or update the cache. For quick feedback we used [optimistic updates: show the result first and roll back if the request fails]."
- **Problems solved:** "[Race conditions: an old response arriving after a new one; cancelling requests when the user leaves; retry rules]."

If you used plain `fetch` in `useEffect`, explain how you handled cleanup and races.
**Why it matters / follow-up:** It tests real-world data handling. Follow-up: "What happens if two components ask for the same data?" (A good cache shares one request.)
**Learn more:** [Data fetching](../../../03-intermediate/02-data-fetching.md)

### R9-PJ-006 (Easy) How did you organise folders, components and styling?
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your project)
- **Folders:** "Grouped by feature (each feature has its components, hooks, tests and API calls together) with a shared folder for generic UI and utilities." Or by type, if that is what you used. Say why it worked or did not.
- **Components:** "Small, focused components. Shared UI components follow one design system. Logic reused in custom hooks."
- **Styling:** "[CSS Modules, Tailwind, CSS-in-JS or plain CSS], because [reason]. Colours and spacing come from design tokens (named variables)."
- **Rules:** "[Naming, import boundaries, lint rules] so a new person finds things easily."

**Why it matters / follow-up:** It shows you care about maintainability. Follow-up: "What problems did the structure cause as the project grew?"
**Learn more:** [Styling](../../../03-intermediate/07-styling.md)

### R9-PJ-007 (Medium) How did you test it, and what did you choose not to test?
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your project)
- **Unit tests** for pure logic (formatters, reducers, helpers).
- **Component tests** with Testing Library: render, act like a user, check what the user sees. Elements are found by role and label, not by class names or internal state. Network calls are mocked at the network level (for example with MSW) so tests stay close to real behaviour.
- **End-to-end tests** (for example Playwright) for the few main flows: sign in, checkout, and so on.
- **CI:** tests run on every pull request.
- **What we skipped:** implementation details, third-party library internals, and snapshot tests of big trees, because they break for no good reason. Say what is true for you.

Name one bug a test caught.
**Why it matters / follow-up:** It checks that you test behaviour, not code structure. Follow-up: "What is your coverage target and what does it miss?" (Coverage shows which lines ran, not whether the behaviour is right.)
**Learn more:** [Testing React](../../../04-advanced/08-testing-react.md)

### R9-PJ-008 (Medium) How did you measure performance? Give a before and after example
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your project) The key rule: **measure first, change one thing, measure again**.
- **Find the problem:** use the browser Performance panel and Lighthouse (lab tests on your machine), the React DevTools Profiler (which components render, and for how long), and field data from real users (Web Vitals: LCP for loading, INP for responsiveness, CLS for layout shift; "good" values are commonly cited as LCP up to 2.5 s, INP up to 200 ms, CLS up to 0.1).
- **Example shape:** "The [page] had [metric] of [before value] on [device or network]. The profile showed [cause, for example a big bundle, a long list, an expensive render]. I changed [one thing: code splitting, list virtualization, moving state down, image sizes]. The metric became [after value]. I checked field data a week later."
- **Be honest:** say if the gain was small or if lab and field data disagreed.

Never quote numbers you do not have.
**Why it matters / follow-up:** Interviewers distrust "I made it faster" without numbers. Follow-up: "Why not wrap everything in `useMemo`?" (It adds cost and complexity, so use it where a measurement shows a problem.)
**Learn more:** [Performance optimization](../../../04-advanced/02-performance-optimization.md)

### R9-PJ-009 (Hard) The hardest bug in the project
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your story) Tell it as a detective story: symptom, hypotheses, evidence, cause, fix, prevention.
- **Symptom:** "Users saw [behaviour], only when [condition]."
- **Reproduce:** "I found steps that made it happen every time, or added logging to catch it."
- **Hypotheses and tests:** "I guessed [A] and checked it by [action]; it was not that. Then [B]..."
- **Root cause:** "[Real cause]." For example (a typical class of React bug, example only): a timer callback used an old copy of state because the effect did not list the state in its dependencies, so the screen showed stale data.
- **Fix and prevention:** "I fixed [what], added a test that failed before, and added [a lint rule or a code-review habit]."

**Why it matters / follow-up:** It shows debugging method and depth. Follow-up: "What would you do differently to find it faster?"
**Learn more:** [useEffect](../../../02-hooks/03-useeffect.md)

### R9-PJ-010 (Medium) If you started again today, what would you change?
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your project) Choose two or three real changes with reasons. Show that you learned from the project and that you understand the earlier choices were reasonable at the time.
- "I would [a specific change, for example write the API types first / add tests for the checkout earlier / move server data to a data library] because [what happened in the project]."
- "I would keep [something that worked well] because [evidence]."

Avoid "I would rewrite everything" and avoid blaming people.
**Why it matters / follow-up:** It tests reflection and honesty. Follow-up: "Why didn't you do it then?" (Answer with the real constraint: time, knowledge, or a deadline.)
**Learn more:** [How React interviews work](../../../00-start-here/03-how-react-interviews-work.md)

### R9-PJ-011 (Medium) How did you handle errors and monitoring in production?
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your project)
- **Render errors:** error boundaries show a fallback screen instead of a blank page. React 19 also lets you pass `onCaughtError`, `onUncaughtError` and `onRecoverableError` options when creating the root, to send errors to a reporting service.
- **Async errors:** errors from requests are not caught by error boundaries by themselves, so each request has an error state, a retry option and a clear message.
- **Monitoring:** an error-monitoring tool collects errors with the release version. Production code is minified, so we upload source maps to the tool (not exposed publicly by accident) to read readable stack traces.
- **Alerts and metrics:** alerts on error rate, and Web Vitals from real users.

Say which parts you truly had.
**Why it matters / follow-up:** It checks production thinking. Follow-up: "How do you avoid alert noise?" (Group errors, set thresholds, and ignore known browser extension errors.)
**Learn more:** [Error boundaries, portals and friends](../../../03-intermediate/05-error-boundaries-portals-and-friends.md)

### R9-PJ-012 (Medium) Authentication and basic security on the frontend
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your project) Say what the project did, then show you know the principles.
- **Sign-in flow:** "[Session cookie or token flow]." A common safe choice is a session cookie with the `HttpOnly`, `Secure` and `SameSite` flags, because JavaScript cannot read an `HttpOnly` cookie, so a script injected by an attacker cannot steal it. Tokens in `localStorage` can be read by any injected script.
- **Authorization:** hiding a button is only for the user experience. The server must check permissions on every request.
- **XSS (cross-site scripting, malicious script in the page):** React escapes text by default. Be careful with `dangerouslySetInnerHTML` and with URLs from users. Sanitise any HTML you must show.
- **Other:** keep secrets out of the frontend bundle (anything in browser code is public), keep dependencies updated.

**Why it matters / follow-up:** It checks security awareness. Follow-up: "What is CSRF and does your setup need protection?" (If you use cookies, yes: SameSite cookies and server-side checks help.)
**Learn more:** [Security and i18n](../../../04-advanced/10-security-and-i18n.md)

### R9-PJ-013 (Medium) From pull request to production: describe the pipeline
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your project)
1. **Branch and pull request:** small changes, reviewed by at least one person.
2. **Automatic checks (CI):** install, lint, type check, tests, build. A failed check blocks the merge.
3. **Preview:** a temporary deployment of the branch for review, if your setup has one.
4. **Merge and release:** automatic or manual deploy to [staging, then production]. Environment settings come from environment variables. In Vite, only variables whose names start with `VITE_` are exposed to browser code, so secrets must never use that prefix.
5. **After release:** watch monitoring. Use feature flags or a gradual rollout for risky changes, and know the rollback step.

**Why it matters / follow-up:** It shows you understand delivery, not only coding. Follow-up: "How long did a change take to reach users, and how would you shorten it?"
**Learn more:** Tooling: Vite, ESLint and build (planned)

### R9-PJ-014 (Hard) A migration or big upgrade: keeping the product working
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your story)
- **Why:** "We migrated from [old] to [new] because [real reason, for example Create React App is no longer maintained, or slow builds, or fewer bugs with types]."
- **Plan:** small steps, not a big bang. Tests first for the risky areas. Run old and new side by side where possible. For JavaScript to TypeScript, for example, convert file by file and allow both during the transition. For a React upgrade, follow the official upgrade guide, fix warnings, and upgrade libraries that depend on React.
- **Safety:** release in small pull requests, use feature flags or gradual rollout, and keep a rollback path.
- **Result:** "[Measured result, for example build time or fewer errors]. Lesson: [what you would do differently]."

**Why it matters / follow-up:** It tests planning and risk management. Follow-up: "How did you convince the team?" (Evidence, a pilot and a clear cost.)
**Learn more:** Tooling: Vite, ESLint and build (planned)

### R9-PJ-015 (Hard) If users, data or the team grew ten times, what would break first?
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your project) Think in three areas and use your project's real weak spots.
- **Users and data:** long lists (use pagination or virtualization), big bundles (code splitting), slow APIs (caching, fewer requests), heavy pages (server rendering or streaming where it helps).
- **Code:** shared state that causes many re-renders, components that do too much, missing types or tests that make changes risky.
- **Team:** slow CI, merge conflicts, unclear ownership. Fixes: a shared design system, clear module boundaries, a monorepo with good tooling, and only if teams need separate releases, split into independently deployed parts (micro-frontends), which add real cost.
- Say what you would measure first. Do not redesign everything.

**Why it matters / follow-up:** It tests system thinking from experience. Follow-up: "What is the first change you would make, and how will you know it worked?"
**Learn more:** [Frontend system design](../../../04-advanced/11-frontend-system-design.md)

## Questions to ask the interviewer

Ask two or three, not all ten. Pick questions you honestly want answered and that fit the person: an engineer, a manager or a recruiter. Listen to the answer and ask a follow-up. Do not ask what you could find on their website, and do not start with salary or holidays in a technical round.

### R9-QA-001 (Easy) A good question about the first months
**Answer:** "What would success look like in the first 90 days, and in the first year?" It shows you think about results. Listen for clear goals. A red flag is an answer like "we will see", with no expectations.
**Why it matters / follow-up:** A follow-up could be "What would a great first project be?" It also tells you how well the team has thought about onboarding.
**Learn more:** [How React interviews work](../../../00-start-here/03-how-react-interviews-work.md)

### R9-QA-002 (Easy) How work is planned, reviewed and released
**Answer:** "How does a feature go from an idea to production here? How are tasks planned, reviewed and released, and how often do you deploy?" Listen for a clear process, small changes, review habits and release speed. Red flag: releases are rare, scary or manual with no one owning them.
**Why it matters / follow-up:** Follow up with "What slows you down the most?"
**Learn more:** Tooling: Vite, ESLint and build (planned)

### R9-QA-003 (Easy) How the team keeps quality high
**Answer:** "How do you test and review code? What do you do when a bug reaches production?" Listen for code review, automated tests, CI, monitoring and a blameless culture. Red flag: "we do not have time for tests" or blame on individuals.
**Why it matters / follow-up:** It shows you care about quality and tells you about the daily reality.
**Learn more:** [Testing React](../../../04-advanced/08-testing-react.md)

### R9-QA-004 (Easy) The frontend stack and its future
**Answer:** "What does the frontend stack look like (React version, framework, TypeScript, state and data tools)? Is there an upgrade or migration planned?" Listen for honest answers about old code and plans. Old stacks are not always bad, but you should know what you will work on, for example class components or a very old React version.
**Why it matters / follow-up:** Good follow-up: "How do you decide when to adopt a new tool?"
**Learn more:** Legacy class components and Redux connect (planned)

### R9-QA-005 (Easy) How design and engineering work together
**Answer:** "How do designers and engineers work together here? Is there a design system, and who maintains it?" Listen for early collaboration, shared components and clear handoff. Red flag: designs are thrown over the wall and engineers are blamed for the gaps.
**Why it matters / follow-up:** It matches the designer-disagreement stories you prepared and shows user focus.
**Learn more:** [Accessibility](../../../04-advanced/09-accessibility.md)

### R9-QA-006 (Medium) The team's real challenges
**Answer:** "What is the biggest challenge the team faces right now? What would you like a new person to help with?" It invites honesty and shows you want to help. Listen for specific problems. A vague answer may mean the problems are not understood, or are not shared with candidates.
**Why it matters / follow-up:** You can then link your own experience: "I have worked on something similar when [your real story]."
**Learn more:** [How React interviews work](../../../00-start-here/03-how-react-interviews-work.md)

### R9-QA-007 (Medium) Incidents, on-call and production problems
**Answer:** "What happens when production breaks? Is there an on-call rota for frontend engineers, and how are incidents reviewed afterwards?" Listen for a calm process, fair rotation, and postmortems that lead to actions. It is also fair to ask how often people are paged.
**Why it matters / follow-up:** It reveals workload and culture. It fits well after interviewers describe the product.
**Learn more:** [Frontend system design](../../../04-advanced/11-frontend-system-design.md)

### R9-QA-008 (Easy) Learning, feedback and growth
**Answer:** "How do engineers get feedback and grow here? How do people move from this level to the next one?" Listen for regular feedback, mentoring and clear expectations. Ask about learning time or a budget only if it matters to you.
**Why it matters / follow-up:** It shows long-term interest. Follow up with "Can you share an example of someone who grew in this team?"
**Learn more:** [How React interviews work](../../../00-start-here/03-how-react-interviews-work.md)

### R9-QA-009 (Medium) A closing question that lets you answer the interviewer's doubts
**Answer:** "Based on our conversation, is there anything about my experience that makes you hesitate? I would like the chance to answer it." It takes some courage. It gives you a chance to correct a misunderstanding while you are still in the room. Stay calm, answer briefly with facts, and do not argue.
**Why it matters / follow-up:** Interviewers often remember candidates who ask for feedback. If there is no time, send a short, polite note afterwards.
**Learn more:** [How React interviews work](../../../00-start-here/03-how-react-interviews-work.md)

### R9-QA-010 (Easy) The hiring process itself
**Answer:** "What are the next steps and the expected timeline? Is there anything I can prepare for the next round?" It shows interest and helps you plan. Write down the names and the format of the next round. Thank them by name.
**Why it matters / follow-up:** Do not ask about salary here unless the interviewer is a recruiter and the topic came up. Use the HR questions below for that.
**Learn more:** [How React interviews work](../../../00-start-here/03-how-react-interviews-work.md)

## HR and salary questions

Rules for all of these: be honest, be calm and be brief. Do not invent numbers, offers or reasons. You can decline to share something, politely, but do not lie. The model answers use placeholders for numbers, because your contract, your market and your goals are specific to you.

### R9-HR-001 (Easy) What is your notice period, and can it be shortened?
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your facts) Read your employment contract first. Then say: "My notice period is [what the contract says]. I could start on [date] if I resign by [date]." If asked about shortening it: "It may be possible to agree an earlier date with my employer. I do not want to promise it before I have talked to them, but I will ask as soon as I have an offer." Do not promise an early release you cannot deliver. Some employers can agree an early release or a payment in place of notice, and some cannot, so check yours.
**Why it matters / follow-up:** The recruiter plans the start date. Follow-up: "Can you work part-time for the other company during notice?" (Check your contract and be open about conflicts of interest.)
**Learn more:** [How React interviews work](../../../00-start-here/03-how-react-interviews-work.md)

### R9-HR-002 (Medium) What are your salary expectations?
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your research) Prepare before the call.
- Research: look at the salary range in the job post (many posts include one), public salary data for your role, level and location, and ask peers.
- Think in total pay, not only the base: bonus, equity, benefits, remote work and growth.
- Safe answers: "I would like to learn more about the role and level first. Could you share the budgeted range?" If they ask you to go first: "Based on my research for this role and location, I am looking at [your researched range]. I am flexible depending on the whole package."
- The range should be true for you: the bottom should be a number you would really accept.

Do not state a number you made up, and do not give a number just to end the awkward moment.
**Why it matters / follow-up:** The first number can set the anchor for the talk, so prepare it. Follow-up: "What if our range is lower?" (Ask what drives the range, whether it has room for experience, and say honestly if it does not work for you.)
**Learn more:** [How React interviews work](../../../00-start-here/03-how-react-interviews-work.md)

### R9-HR-003 (Medium) Why are you leaving your current job?
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your reason) Say a true reason in a positive, forward-looking way. Examples of honest, safe reasons: "I want more ownership of architecture," "I have learned what I can in this role," "The company changed direction," "I want to work on [type of product]," or "The company had layoffs." Pattern: "I am grateful for [something real]. I am now looking for [what you want], and this role offers [what you read about it]." Do not speak badly about your manager or colleagues, even if they were difficult. Do not invent a reason.
**Why it matters / follow-up:** The interviewer checks attitude and whether you will leave them for the same reason. Follow-up: "What would make you stay in a job?"
**Learn more:** [How React interviews work](../../../00-start-here/03-how-react-interviews-work.md)

### R9-HR-004 (Medium) Do you have other offers, or are you interviewing elsewhere?
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your facts) Tell the truth. If you are interviewing elsewhere: "Yes, I am talking with a few companies. This role is [a real reason it interests you]. My timeline is [honest timeline]." If you have an offer, you can say you have one and when it expires, because that helps the recruiter plan. If you have none, say "I am in early conversations" and nothing more. Never invent an offer or inflate its value to gain leverage: it is easy to get caught, and it can end the process.
**Why it matters / follow-up:** The company wants to know the timeline and how serious you are. Follow-up: "Can you give us an answer by [date]?" (Ask politely for time you really need.)
**Learn more:** [How React interviews work](../../../00-start-here/03-how-react-interviews-work.md)

### R9-HR-005 (Medium) A gap in the CV or a very short stay
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your facts) Be short, factual and calm. "From [month] to [month] I [real reason: studied, took care of family, had a health issue, was laid off, took a break]. During that time I [anything true and useful: learned, built a project, volunteered]. I am ready and excited to work again." You do not have to share private health or family details: "a personal reason" is enough. For a short stay: "The role was not what was described: [neutral facts]. I learned [lesson], and I now ask about [what you check before joining]."
**Why it matters / follow-up:** The interviewer wants a believable explanation, not a perfect record. Follow-up: "How have you kept your skills up to date?" Have one real example.
**Learn more:** [How React interviews work](../../../00-start-here/03-how-react-interviews-work.md)

### R9-HR-006 (Easy) Where do you see yourself in three to five years?
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your goals) Describe direction, not a job title. "I want to grow into [a larger scope, for example owning the architecture of a product area, or leading a small team, or becoming an expert in performance or accessibility]. I want to keep learning and to be someone others ask for advice." Choose what is true for you: both the technical (senior or staff) path and the people-management path are valid. Link it to the role: "This job would help me because [reason]."
**Why it matters / follow-up:** The interviewer checks ambition and fit. A person who wants something this job cannot offer may leave quickly. Follow-up: "What do you need from a manager to get there?"
**Learn more:** [How React interviews work](../../../00-start-here/03-how-react-interviews-work.md)

### R9-HR-007 (Easy) Relocation, on-site work or a different time zone
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your facts) Say what is possible, what is not and what depends. "I can work [fully remote / hybrid / on site] in [city]. I [can / cannot] relocate before [date]. For time zones, I can overlap with the team for [hours] a day." Ask about the real arrangement: how many office days, who pays for relocation, and how meetings are scheduled across zones. Do not say yes to something you will not do just to pass the interview.
**Why it matters / follow-up:** A mismatch discovered after the offer wastes everyone's time. Follow-up: "Would you accept a trial period on site?"
**Learn more:** [How React interviews work](../../../00-start-here/03-how-react-interviews-work.md)

### R9-HR-008 (Medium) What is your current salary?
**Answer:** (example outline, replace with your choice) You have options, and the rules depend on where you live. In some places, employers are not allowed to ask about pay history, so check the rules in your country or region. You may politely decline: "I would prefer to focus on the value of this role. My expectation is [your researched range]." If you decide to share, share the truth, because in some markets employers can verify it with payslips or a background check. Never give a false number. Be clear whether you mean base pay or total pay.
**Why it matters / follow-up:** Some recruiters use it to set an offer. Follow-up: "We need it for our records." (Ask what the records are used for, and give accurate information if you agree.)
**Learn more:** [How React interviews work](../../../00-start-here/03-how-react-interviews-work.md)
